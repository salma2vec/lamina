# From build sheet to cane

This guide tells you how to make a nerikomi cane from a Lamina build sheet. It also tells you how to test your clays, what the build-sheet numbers mean, and how to work safely.

## 1. Terms

- **Nerikomi** (練り込み) is a Japanese method of building patterns from clays of different colours. The pattern runs through a block of clay, like a millefiori glass cane. You cut thin slices from the block and assemble them into a piece. [1] The term came into common use in the 1970s. [1]
- **Neriage** (練上げ) usually means coloured clays thrown on the wheel. "Age" refers to the pulling-up action of throwing. "Komi" means to press in, as in pressing slabs into a mould. [2]
- **Agateware** is the English term for wares made from more than one clay colour, from early Stoke-on-Trent production. [1]

Lamina designs are for nerikomi: hand-built canes, sliced and assembled.

## 2. Build a cane

### 2.1 Prepare the coloured clays

1. Weigh each clay as the build sheet specifies. The masses include 10% for waste.
2. To stain a clay, weigh the dry stain. Make a well in the clay, add the stain and a little water to make a paste, then wedge until the colour is even. [3] Some potters mix the dry stain into dry clay first, then add water, and let the clay stiffen on a plaster bat before wedging. [4]
3. Wedge each colour until it is smooth and has no air bubbles. [4]
4. Bring all colours to the same softness. If one clay is wetter or drier, let it rest until it matches. [4] Clays of different stiffness distort when you compress and reduce the cane.
5. Wrap each colour in plastic and let it rest. Water distributes evenly during rest. [2]

Wear gloves and a respirator when you handle dry stain. See section 7.

### 2.2 Build the cross-section from the template

1. Print the 1:1 SVG template at 100% scale. Do not use "fit to page". Measure the scale bar on the print. If it does not match, adjust the print settings and print again.
2. Cut slabs to thickness with wooden guide slats and a rolling pin, or with fishing line. [3]
3. Cut shapes from the slabs to match the template regions. Use coils or rolled logs for round regions. Use stacked slabs for layered regions.
4. Build from the inside out. The pattern only shows when you cut the block. [4]
5. Join pieces with a little water or slip. Wet clay of the same softness joins without seams. [3]
6. Compress the block after each stage. Use a rolling pin, or drop the block flat on the table. Compression bonds the colours and removes air. Too much pressure distorts the design. [3][4]
7. Wrap the finished block in plastic.

### 2.3 Reduce the cane

Reduction makes the cross-section smaller and the cane longer. The pattern becomes finer.

- **Rolling or pulling.** Roll the cane on the table with flat hands, from the centre to the ends. Work slowly. Complex patterns distort from end to end. [5]
- **Squeezing.** Press the cane on all sides between thumbs and fingers held at right angles. This keeps a square section and keeps the pattern more intact. [5]
- **Joining canes.** Shapes that tile (squares, hexagons) can be packed into a larger block and reduced again. [5]

The ends of a reduced cane distort most. Trim them off. The 10% waste allowance on the build sheet covers this for most canes. Add more for long reductions.

### 2.4 Slice and assemble

1. Let the cane firm up a little, so it holds its shape under the wire. Do not let it go dry. [4]
2. Cut slices with a thin wire. Use guide slats for an even thickness. [3]
3. Keep slices covered under plastic or between damp cloths. Thin slices crack if they are bent or dry quickly. [3][4]
4. Assemble slices with one of these methods:
   - **On a slab.** Lay slices on a thin base slab. Roll with a cloth between the pin and the clay. Remove the cloth when the surface is dry enough that the pin does not drag colour. Wipe the pin after each pass. [3]
   - **In a press mould.** Press slices edge to edge into a hump or slump mould. [4]
   - **Edge to edge.** Score and slip the seams, then compress them. [4]

### 2.5 Dry and fire

1. Dry slowly under plastic for several days or weeks. Uneven drying is a common cause of failure. [4] Different clays lose water at different rates. Slow drying lets the moisture even out. [3]
2. Bisque fire as normal for your clay. [4]
3. Wet-sand the bisque to clean the pattern and remove smeared colour. See 2.6.
4. Fire to maturity. Fire every clay in the piece to the same cone you used for your tests.

### 2.6 Sand and polish unglazed surfaces

- Sand at the bisque stage when you can. Bisque is softer and paper lasts longer. [6]
- Use wet/dry silicon carbide paper, soaked in water, on dampened ware. [7] Potters report 100 to 220 grit for bisque. [6]
- After the final firing, use diamond hand pads with water for any rough areas. [6] Water keeps the dust down and reduces pad wear. [8]
- For small rounded pieces, a rock tumbler with water and aluminium oxide grit is an option. [6]
- Never sand dry. See section 7.

## 3. Clay compatibility

### 3.1 Why clays crack apart

Every clay shrinks when it dries and again when it fires. If two clays in one piece shrink by different amounts, stress builds at the boundary. The piece can crack in the kiln, or weeks later. Thermal expansion also matters. If the clays expand differently on heating and cooling, the piece can fail under rapid temperature change. [9]

The boundary between two clays is where failure happens. Digitalfire shows a bowl that cracked along the line between a more vitreous red porcelain and a less vitreous green porcelain. [9]

Lamina's default palette uses three separate commercial bodies: porcelain, sandalwood stoneware, and volcanic black clay. Separate bodies can differ in recipe, shrinkage and firing range. Test them together before you build a piece.

### 3.2 The safer route: one base body, stained

Divide one porcelain into portions and stain each portion. All colours then share one base recipe, so they shrink at similar rates. [4] Lamina's stained-porcelain preset follows this method.

Stains still change shrinkage. A stain can make a porcelain more or less vitreous. Laminating high- and low-shrinkage versions of one porcelain can still crack. [10] Keep stain loadings as low as the colour allows, and test. [10]

### 3.3 Stain loadings

Sources give different ranges. All of them say: test.

| Source | Loading in clay body |
|---|---|
| Digitalfire | About 2–3% for strong stains such as blues. Above an optimum, more stain adds little colour. [10] |
| Corrie Bain | 5–10% by weight. [4] |
| Ceramic Arts Network, nerikomi blocks | About 10% (½ lb dry stain to 5 lb wet clay). [3] |
| Ceramic Arts Network, pigments | 10–15%. [11] |
| Good Earth Clays | Start at 10–20%. Darks (black, blue, green) need less. Lights (pink, yellow) need more. [12] |

Note the basis. Some sources weigh stain against wet clay [3], others against "clay" without saying wet or dry [12]. Record which basis you use.

### 3.4 Test 1: shrinkage bars

Make one set of bars for each clay, including each stained version.

1. Roll each clay to about 10 mm (⅜") thick between guide rods. [13]
2. Cut bars. Press two marks exactly 100 mm apart into each bar. Mark the clay name on each bar. [13]
3. Dry slowly and evenly so the bars do not warp. [13]
4. Measure the distance between the marks with callipers to 0.1 mm. This is the dry length. [13]
5. Fire to your final cone, next to witness cones. [13]
6. Measure again. This is the fired length.

Calculate (wet length = 100 mm):

```
Drying shrinkage (%) = (wet − dry) / wet × 100
Firing shrinkage (%) = (dry − fired) / dry × 100
Total shrinkage (%)  = (wet − fired) / wet × 100
```

Firing shrinkage uses the dry length as the divisor. For this reason drying and firing shrinkage do not add up to total shrinkage. [13] Enter **total shrinkage** in Lamina.

Make three bars per clay and use the average.

Lamina warns when two clays differ by more than 1.5 percentage points in total shrinkage. This is Lamina's own threshold. It is not a published standard. A smaller difference can still crack. Use the tile test below.

### 3.5 Test 2: laminated test tiles

1. For each pair of clays in your design, stack thin slabs of the two clays. Compress them.
2. Cut slices and roll them into a tile, as in 2.4.
3. Dry, bisque, and fire to the same cone as the bars.
4. Inspect every boundary with a magnifier. Look for cracks, gaps, or a lip where one clay stands proud of the other.
5. Keep the tiles for a few weeks and inspect them again. Stress can release late. [9]

Digitalfire also describes an Engobe Body Compatibility Test for pairs of bodies. [9]

### 3.6 Fire all tests to the same cone

A porcelain and a stoneware can have different maturing temperatures. Fire bars and tiles of all clays together, in one kiln load, to the cone you will use for the finished work. If one clay is over-fired or under-fired at that cone, change the clay.

## 4. Reading the build sheet

### 4.1 Reduction ratio

```
Reduction ratio R = starting cross-section width / final cross-section width
```

- The cross-section area becomes 1/R² of the start.
- Clay volume stays the same, so the cane length becomes R² times the start. A 10 cm block reduced by R = 2 becomes about 40 cm long.
- Real canes do not reduce evenly. Ends distort and the centre and the outside move at different rates. [5]

### 4.2 Minimum feature size

The minimum feature size is the smallest region in the template, after reduction and firing:

```
Fired feature size = template feature size / R × (1 − total shrinkage / 100)
```

If you cannot cut and place a region at template scale, you cannot build it. Increase the template size, reduce R, or use fewer colours.

### 4.3 Mass estimate

```
Mass per clay = region area × cane length × wet density × 1.10
```

- Area and length are at template (pre-reduction) scale.
- The default wet density is 1.9 g/cm³.
- The factor 1.10 adds 10% for waste.

The default is consistent with typical clay: plastic clay at modelling stiffness contains about 19.5–22.5% water by wet weight [14], and clay minerals have a density of about 2.5 g/cm³ [15]. A 100 g lump with 21% water then has a volume of 79/2.5 + 21/1.0 ≈ 52.6 cm³, which gives about 1.9 g/cm³. Air pockets, grog, and stain change this value.

### 4.4 Fired size

```
Fired size = wet size × (1 − total shrinkage / 100)
```

This assumes even shrinkage. Thin slabs can warp, and different clays in one piece shrink by different amounts. Fired size is an estimate.

### 4.5 Measure your own values

**Wet density.**

1. Roll a slab between guide rods and cut a rectangular block with a ruler and knife.
2. Measure length, width and height in cm. Multiply to get the volume.
3. Weigh the block in grams.
4. Density = mass / volume.

For an irregular lump, use water displacement. Fill a measuring jug, note the level, lower the lump in on a thread, and read the new level. Work quickly, because clay absorbs water. Measure each clay at the stiffness you build with.

**Shrinkage.** Use the bars in 3.4. Enter the total shrinkage for each clay in Lamina.

## 5. Translucency

Thin, mature porcelain passes light. Nerikomi in porcelain shows the pattern in both reflected and transmitted light.

- **Thickness.** Reported translucent walls range from about 2 mm to 6 mm, depending on the body. Digitalfire shows a cone 6 porcelain mug translucent at about 3.5 mm walls, and a 2 mm wall made with stiffer clay. [16] A cast porcelain slip for lithophanes is translucent from 3 mm (⅛") to 6 mm (¼"). [17] Test your own porcelain at 1 mm steps.
- **Maturity.** Porcelain becomes translucent when it is fired close enough to melting to take on a glass-like quality. Cone 10 is not required. Cone 6 porcelains can be translucent. [16]
- **Body.** More feldspar increases translucency but reduces stability in the kiln. Kaolins with iron or titanium reduce translucency. [16]
- **Stains.** Stained regions can transmit light differently from unstained porcelain. Make a backlit test tile for each colour at your planned thickness.
- **Warping.** Thin porcelain warps or cracks in the kiln. [17] Dry thin pieces between light plaster bats or under light weights. Fire flat pieces on a shelf dusted with aluminium hydroxide so they can move as they shrink. [17]
- **Drying.** Thin and thick areas dry at different rates. Wrap the piece fully in plastic for about two days to equalise moisture, then dry slowly. [18]

## 6. Glaze and copper-red accents (optional)

### 6.1 Glaze hides the pattern

A thick or opaque glaze covers the pattern. Many nerikomi pieces stay unglazed or have a very thin clear glaze. [4] Polish unglazed surfaces instead (2.6). If a surface must hold food or drink, use a tested clear glaze on that surface. Stained bodies are best avoided as food surfaces. [10]

### 6.2 Copper red in an electric kiln

Copper reds (sang-de-boeuf) usually need a reduction atmosphere. In an electric kiln, you can make reduction inside the glaze by adding silicon carbide (SiC). The SiC releases carbon that reduces the copper. [19][20]

- **Copper.** About 0.25% copper carbonate is enough, though more is often used. One cone 10 example uses 0.5% copper carbonate with 1% tin oxide. Tin helps the red. [19]
- **Silicon carbide.** Reported additions are about 0.2% to 1%. A 1932 study used 0.3% copper carbonate and 1% SiC at cone 9. Other potters use ⅓ to ½%. [21] Use very fine SiC (800 mesh or finer). Coarse SiC causes bubbling. [21]
- **Problems.** SiC causes blisters and pinholes. [19][20] The SiC is used up quickly, so fast firing is required. [20] Results are not consistent between kilns.
- **Flow.** Copper reds run. Where they run off rims and edges, they turn white. [19]

Use copper red as an accent on an area with no pattern, such as a rim, a foot, or the inside of a bowl. Do not glaze over the nerikomi pattern. Test on tiles first and fire on a cookie or waste slab to catch runs.

## 7. Studio safety

### 7.1 Silica dust

Clay contains crystalline silica. Fine dust damages the lungs.

- Never dry-sand or fettle clay. Finish clay when it is wet or leather-hard. [7]
- Add dry materials to water, not water to dry materials. [7]
- Sand bisque only with wet/dry paper soaked in water, on dampened ware. [7]
- Do not sweep dry clay. Wet-mop, or use an H-class vacuum (Australian guidance) [7] or at least an M-class vacuum (UK HSE guidance) [22].
- Never use brushes or compressed air to remove dust from skin, clothes, or surfaces. [22]
- Do not let slip or clay spills dry out. Clean them up while wet. [22]
- Wear a P2 mask or respirator when dust is present. It must fit correctly. Facial hair prevents a good seal. [7]
- Keep work clothes apart from clean clothes. Tell whoever washes them that the dust contains silica. [22]

### 7.2 Stains and oxides

- Read the safety data sheet (SDS) for each stain before you use it. Hazards differ between pigments. [11]
- Some stains contain carcinogenic metals. For example, the SDS for Mason 6600 Black lists chrome, cobalt, iron and nickel oxides. It lists nickel oxide as a confirmed human carcinogen (ACGIH). [23]
- Wear gloves and a respirator when you mix dry stain into clay. [3]
- Keep stain work as dust-free as possible. [12]
- A polished surface that contains cadmium or another hazardous inclusion pigment is not suitable for food or drink. [10]

### 7.3 Silicon carbide

Treat SiC as a fine abrasive dust. Weigh and mix it wet, with a respirator.
